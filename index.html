OODL

Signed Bitcoin offers, published on-chain and filled by anyone. The seller's price is fixed by their signature, and Bitcoin settles both sides in one transaction.

Protocol version 1.0. Works on Bitcoin today, with no soft fork. Written by switch-900.

Trade on oodinals.com Read the specification

Abstract

A seller signs a partially signed Bitcoin transaction that pays them a fixed price for an inscription, and publishes it inside a Bitcoin transaction. Anyone who finds the offer can complete it by adding their own inputs and outputs, then broadcast it. The seller's signature commits to their payment. Bitcoin consensus guarantees that the inscription and the payment move together, or not at all.

There is no marketplace operator, no escrow and no account. The only signature construction involved, SIGHASH_SINGLE | ANYONECANPAY, has been part of Bitcoin since 2009.

1. Why publish offers on-chain

Bitcoin has always supported half-finished transactions that someone else completes later. What it never had was a neutral place to put them. So trading moved to marketplaces that keep the order book in their own database, where listings can be delisted, censored or lost when the platform shuts down.

OODL puts the offer itself on Bitcoin. Any indexer can find it, any wallet can fill it, and no one can remove it except the seller.

2. How a listing works

Every listing uses three transactions.

  1. Commit. Moves the inscription to sat offset zero in a Taproot output and pays the listing fee.
  2. Reveal. Publishes the seller's signed PSBT in witness data, inside an envelope tagged oodl. Nothing is sold yet. The offer is now public.
  3. Buy. Anyone extracts the PSBT, adds their inputs and outputs, signs their own inputs and broadcasts.

The reveal envelope follows the same pattern as an inscription:

OP_FALSE
OP_IF
  "oodl"
  0x01                               # protocol version
  "Content-Type" "application/psbt"
  0x00                               # compression flag
  <signed PSBT bytes>               # split into 520-byte pushes
OP_ENDIF

To cancel, the seller spends the listed UTXO. Every copy of the offer, on-chain or shared privately, becomes invalid at once.

3. The buy transaction

SIGHASH_SINGLE means the seller's signature covers their own input and the output at the same position: here, the inscription in and 1,250,000 sats out. Changing either one breaks the signature. ANYONECANPAY means the signature ignores every other input, so a buyer who didn't exist when the offer was signed can add theirs later.

Ordinal theory tracks sats in order, first in, first out. The buyer puts two small padding inputs first and merges them into output 1 at exactly the same value. That places the inscription's sat at the very start of output 2, the buyer's receiver. Wallets should simulate this before signing.

5. Who takes part

Sellers
Sign an offer and publish it. No buyer needs to exist yet.
Executors
Find offers, add funding and broadcast. Anyone can do it.
Wallets
Verify offers, check sat flow and sign. The interface most people use.
Indexers
Scan Bitcoin for listings and serve them to wallets. Anyone can run one.
Miners
Confirm settlement. A miner can also act as an executor, at the same signed price as anyone else.

6. Security

OODL adds no trust assumptions to settlement. Each guarantee comes from Bitcoin consensus.

Fixed payout
The seller's signature fixes their payment. No executor can redirect it without invalidating the signature.
Checks before signing
Wallets confirm the seller's signature, check the listed input is unspent and simulate sat flow before the buyer signs anything.
Tamper evidence
Any change to the seller's signed input or output is rejected by every node.
Unilateral cancellation
Spending the listed UTXO kills the offer everywhere, with no permission needed.
Atomic settlement
The inscription and the payment move in one transaction. There is no state where one side has been paid and the other hasn't.

7. What it can't do yet

With today's signature flags, a seller can enforce the payment they receive and nothing more. That rules out:

  • Asset-for-asset swaps. A seller can require bitcoin in return, but not a specific inscription or rune, because sat flow depends on inputs the seller doesn't sign.
  • Trustless bids. Buy orders and collection offers can't guarantee the buyer receives the asset.
  • Royalties at sale. Extra outputs can't be enforced when a listing is filled. OODL collects its fee at listing time instead.
  • Automatic expiry. nLockTime can delay when an offer becomes valid, but nothing can end it. A listing lasts until the seller spends the UTXO.
  • Partial fills. Each offer is all or nothing, so fungible assets trade in fixed lots.
  • Anything off-chain. Bitcoin can only enforce what it can see. Fiat, physical goods and delivered work are out of scope.

Covenant proposals such as OP_CTV and OP_CAT would lift several of these. The envelope carries a version byte, so new offer types can be added if they ever activate.

8. Build with it

The Oodinals-Nexus Loader is a single inscribed JavaScript module. One import gives you wallet connection, inscribing, listing, buying and a listing indexer, with no npm and no build step.

// load the SDK from Bitcoin
const { default: Nexus } = await import(
  '/r/sat/534764996708771/at/-1/content'
);

await Nexus.connectWallet('unisat');
const state = Nexus.getWalletState();
const utxos = await Nexus.getSpendableUtxos([state.paymentAddress]);

// fill a listing
await Nexus.buyFromInscriptionId({
  inscriptionId: '<txid>i0',
  buyerPaymentUtxos: utxos,
  buyerChangeAddress: state.paymentAddress,
  buyerReceiveAddress: state.ordinalsAddress,
  feeRateSatPerVb: 10,
  broadcast: true
});
  • Supports UniSat, Xverse, OKX, Leather, Phantom, Wizz, OYL and Bitmap Wallet.
  • The import above follows the latest version on that sat. For code that signs or moves assets, pin an exact inscription with /content/<id> so its behaviour can't change underneath you.
  • A 2,000-sat platform fee is included by default. You can change it or set it to zero on any call.
  • UTXO lookups, fee rates and broadcasting use external APIs through a small popup window.
